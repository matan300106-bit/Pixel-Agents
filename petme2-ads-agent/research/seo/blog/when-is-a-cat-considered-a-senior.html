Your cat may still chase toys and sprint down the hall, so it can be a surprise to hear the word "senior" at the vet. So when is a cat considered a senior? In this guide we share the age ranges most people use, a simple cat age chart, the signs of aging to look for, and easy ways to keep an older cat comfortable, well fed, and well hydrated.

When is a cat considered a senior?

Most cats are considered seniors at around 11 years old. Many vets begin to talk about "senior care" a bit earlier, often from around 7 to 10 years, because that is when some age-related changes can start.

There is no single official birthday when a cat becomes old. Different vets and cat groups use slightly different words and ages. Still, the ranges below are widely used, and they are a helpful way to think about your cat's needs as the years go by.

How old is a senior cat? Cat life-stage age chart

A senior cat is usually about 11 to 14 years old, and a cat 15 or older is often called geriatric. Here is a simple chart of commonly used life stages. Treat it as general guidance only.

Life stage Cat age (commonly used range) What it often looks like
KittenBirth to about 1 yearFast growth, lots of play, many small meals
Young adultAbout 1 to 6 yearsFull size, high energy, settled routines
MatureAbout 7 to 10 yearsOften a bit calmer; weight gain is common
SeniorAbout 11 to 14 yearsMore sleep, slower jumps, changes in appetite or thirst may show up
Geriatric ("super senior")About 15 years and olderNeeds extra comfort, closer watching, and more frequent vet care

Many indoor cats now live well into their teens, so a cat can spend a big part of its life in the mature and senior stages. That is a good reason to plan ahead.

What age is a senior cat in human years?

A common rough comparison puts an 11-year-old cat at around 60 in human years, and a 15-year-old cat at around the mid-70s. Cats age very fast in the first two years, then more slowly after that.

A simple rule of thumb many people use: the first year of a cat's life is like about 15 human years, the second year adds about 9 more (so a 2-year-old cat is about 24), and each year after that adds about 4 human years. It is not exact science, but it helps explain why an 11-year-old cat needs a bit more care.

Signs your cat is getting older

The most common signs of aging are more sleep, less jumping, and small changes in eating, drinking, or grooming. Age itself is not a disease, but these changes are worth noticing.

  • Sleeping more and playing in shorter bursts.
  • Jumping less, or using a chair to "step" up instead of leaping to the counter.
  • Stiffness after naps, or a slower walk on stairs.
  • Grooming changes, such as a scruffy or matted coat on the back and hips.
  • Weight changes, either gain or loss.
  • Appetite changes, like picky eating or eating less at each meal.
  • Thirst or litter box changes, such as drinking more, bigger clumps, or accidents outside the box.
  • Behavior changes, like more meowing at night, seeming confused, or wanting more (or less) company.

Many of these can also be signs of a health problem, not just "getting old." Weight loss, drinking a lot more, eating much less, or litter box changes are all good reasons to book a vet visit soon rather than wait.

How to feed a senior cat

Most senior cats do well with small, measured meals at regular times, and a food your vet agrees is right for them. Older cats can have smaller appetites, and some lose or gain weight more easily.

  1. Ask your vet about the food. Some older cats stay on their usual adult food. Others do better on a senior food or a diet your vet suggests for a health need. Do not switch to a special diet on your own if your cat has a health condition.
  2. Serve small, regular meals. Splitting the daily amount into 3 or 4 small meals can be easier for a cat with a small appetite. It also makes it easier to notice when your cat skips a meal.
  3. Measure portions. Use the label as a starting point, then adjust with your vet based on your cat's weight. Our guide to how much a cat should eat has a simple chart.
  4. Weigh your cat often. A kitchen or baby scale at home makes it easy to check every few weeks. Small, steady changes are easy to miss by eye.
  5. Make food easy to reach. A slightly raised bowl can be more comfortable for a stiff neck or back. Wide, shallow bowls are easier on whiskers.
  6. Change food slowly. When you switch foods, mix the old and new over about a week to avoid an upset tummy.

Wet food can be a helpful part of a senior cat's diet because it adds water. Many owners feed a mix of wet and dry food. Talk with your vet about what balance suits your cat.

Keeping a senior cat hydrated

Older cats need fresh, clean water every day, in places that are easy to reach. Good hydration matters at every age, and it is something vets often watch closely in older cats.

  • Offer more than one water spot, especially on each floor of your home, so your cat does not need to climb stairs to drink.
  • Keep water away from food and the litter box. Many cats prefer to drink a few feet from where they eat.
  • Change water daily and wash bowls often.
  • Try moving water. Some cats drink more readily from a fountain. Learn why in why do cats like running water.
  • Watch for changes. As a general guideline, cats need about 3.5 to 4.5 oz of water per 5 lb of body weight per day, from all sources. See how much water a cat should drink for more. If your cat suddenly drinks much more or much less, call your vet.

Comfort tips for older cats

Small changes around the home can make daily life much easier for a senior cat. Think about how your cat moves, rests, eats, and uses the litter box.

  • Litter box: choose a box with a low side or a cut-out entry, keep it very clean, and put one on each floor.
  • Beds: offer soft, warm beds in quiet spots. Many older cats love a sunny window or a bed near (not on) a heat source.
  • Steps and ramps: pet steps or a sturdy box help your cat reach the couch, bed, or window seat.
  • Grooming help: gentle brushing a few times a week helps with spots your cat can no longer reach well. It is also a nice time to feel for lumps.
  • Nail checks: older cats may scratch less, so nails can grow long. Check them often.
  • Gentle play: short, easy play sessions with wand toys keep the body and mind active.
  • Calm routine: keep meals, play, and bedtime at about the same times each day. Many older cats find routine reassuring.

How often should a senior cat see the vet?

Many vets suggest check-ups about every 6 months for senior cats, but your vet will tell you what is right for your cat. Older cats can change quickly, and regular visits help catch problems early.

At a senior visit, your vet may check weight, teeth, joints, heart, and blood pressure, and may suggest blood and urine tests. Bring notes about anything new you have seen at home, such as changes in eating, drinking, sleeping, or the litter box. A short weekly note on your phone makes this easy.

Call your vet soon (do not wait for the next check-up) if your cat:

  • Stops eating, or eats much less, for more than a day.
  • Loses weight without a clear reason.
  • Drinks or urinates much more than usual.
  • Vomits often, has diarrhea, or seems to be in pain.
  • Has trouble breathing, walking, or using the litter box.
  • Seems confused, hides much more, or suddenly changes behavior.

Simple daily routine for a senior cat

A calm, steady routine is one of the easiest ways to support an older cat. Here is an example to adapt with your vet:

  1. Morning: small breakfast, fresh water, quick look at the litter box.
  2. Midday: a small second meal (an automatic feeder can serve this if you are out), plus a sunny nap spot.
  3. Afternoon: a few minutes of gentle play or brushing.
  4. Evening: dinner, then a small late meal if your vet agrees, so long gaps between meals are avoided.
  5. Weekly: weigh your cat, check nails and teeth, and wash food and water bowls (or clean the fountain) as needed.

Frequently asked questions

When is a cat considered a senior?

A cat is usually considered a senior at around 11 years old. Many vets start talking about senior care from about 7 to 10 years, the "mature" stage, because some age changes can begin then. Your vet can tell you which stage fits your cat.

How old is a senior cat?

A senior cat is commonly described as about 11 to 14 years old. Cats about 15 and older are often called geriatric or "super senior." These are general ranges, and every cat ages a little differently.

What age is a senior cat in human years?

As a rough comparison, an 11-year-old cat is often said to be about 60 in human years, and a 15-year-old cat about the mid-70s. A common rule of thumb counts the first cat year as about 15 human years, the second as about 9 more, and each later year as about 4. It is an estimate, not an exact formula.

Is a 7-year-old cat a senior?

A 7-year-old cat is usually called "mature" rather than senior. Many vets still suggest paying closer attention to weight, teeth, and health from around this age. Most cats are considered seniors at around 11.

Is a 10-year-old cat old?

A 10-year-old cat is generally in the mature stage, close to senior. Many cats this age are still active and healthy. It is a good time to ask your vet about senior check-ups and any diet changes.

What is the average lifespan of an indoor cat?

Many indoor cats live into their mid-teens, and some live past 20. Lifespan depends on genetics, diet, weight, and health care. Regular vet visits and a healthy weight help give a cat the best chance at a long life.

Should I switch my cat to senior cat food?

Not every older cat needs senior food, so ask your vet first. Some healthy seniors do fine on their usual adult food, while others benefit from a senior formula or a diet for a specific health need. If you switch, mix old and new food over about a week.

How often should I feed a senior cat?

Many senior cats do well with 3 or 4 small meals a day made from their daily amount. Small, regular meals can suit a smaller appetite and make it easier to notice changes. Ask your vet how much your cat should eat in total.

How much water should a senior cat drink?

A general guideline for cats is about 3.5 to 4.5 oz of water per 5 lb of body weight per day, including water from food. Cats on wet food often drink less from the bowl. A big change in how much your senior cat drinks is a reason to call your vet.

Why is my senior cat drinking so much water?

Drinking much more than usual can be a sign of a health problem in older cats, so it is worth a vet visit. Hot weather or a switch to more dry food can also raise thirst. Your vet may suggest blood and urine tests to find the cause.

Why is my older cat losing weight?

Weight loss in a senior cat can have many causes, including dental pain, illness, or simply eating less. It is not a normal part of aging to ignore. Weigh your cat regularly and see your vet if you notice a drop.

How often should a senior cat go to the vet?

Many vets suggest a check-up about every 6 months for senior cats. Your vet may recommend a different schedule based on your cat's health. Call sooner if you notice changes in eating, drinking, weight, or behavior.

What are signs of old age in cats?

Common signs include sleeping more, jumping less, stiffness, a scruffy coat, and changes in appetite or weight. Some older cats also meow more at night or seem confused. Because these can also signal health issues, mention them to your vet.

Can an automatic feeder help a senior cat?

Yes, an automatic feeder can serve small, measured dry food meals on time, which suits many older cats. It keeps a steady routine when you are busy or away. PETME2 feeders are for dry food only, so plan wet meals separately.
